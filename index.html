Warm-up

AI coding tools let one programmer do the work of two.

So what happens to the number of programmers employed? Make your prediction. We'll come back to it at the end.

Big idea #1

Labor demand is a derived demand

Nobody wants programmers for their own sake. Firms hire programmers because consumers want software.

The demand for any factor of production (labor, capital, land) is derived from the demand for the good or service it produces.

So the real question is:

When programmers become more productive, what happens to the market for software? Labor follows from that.

Toolkit

Three formulas you need

Marginal Product (MP)

Extra output from hiring one more worker.

MP = ΔTP / ΔL

Falls as more workers are added (diminishing marginal returns).

Marginal Revenue Product (MRP)

Extra revenue from hiring one more worker.

MRP = MP × P

This holds when the firm sells in a perfectly competitive product market (P = MR). The MRP curve is the firm's labor demand curve.

Marginal Resource Cost (MRC)

Extra cost of hiring one more worker.

MRC = W

This holds in a perfectly competitive labor market, where the firm is a wage-taker.

Profit-maximizing hiring rule: keep hiring until MRP = MRC

The trap

The "fixed amount of work" mistake

The naive argument goes like this:

"CodeCo makes 45 features with 6 programmers. With AI, 3 programmers make 54 features. So CodeCo will fire half its staff."

The hidden assumption is that the amount of software the world wants is fixed. Economists call this the lump-of-labor fallacy.

At 2× productivity (P = $1,000, W = $5,000), every programmer's MRP doubles. The 7th and 8th programmers now bring in $8,000 and $6,000, more than the $5,000 they cost. CodeCo hires 8, not 3, and produces 104 features. If the price holds, that is.

Force #1 · pushes employment UP

Higher productivity raises MRP

AI tool→MP ↑→MRP = MP × P ↑→DL shifts right→W ↑ and L ↑

Every programmer now adds more output per hour, so each one is worth more to the firm. At any given wage, firms want more programmers, not fewer.

AP exam tip

Unless the question says the product price changes, the standard AP answer is: productivity ↑ → MP ↑ → MRP ↑ → labor demand shifts right. Productivity is one of the shifters of labor demand.

Force #2 · can push employment DOWN

But cheaper production lowers the price of software

Productivity ↑→cost per feature ↓→supply of software shifts right→P ↓, Q ↑

When every firm adopts the tool, the supply of software increases and the market price falls. Since MRP = MP × P, a falling P pulls MRP back down.

The question that decides the outcome:

When software gets cheaper, how much more of it do people buy?

That's the price elasticity of demand for software, a Unit 2 idea that comes back in Unit 5.

The hinge

Elasticity decides who wins

Here's a handy identity: programmers needed = software output ÷ output per programmer. So employment rises only if output grows faster than productivity.

%Δ programmers ≈ %Δ software output − %Δ productivity

Example: productivity +50%, so the software price falls about 33%.

Why software demand tends to be elastic: when building software gets cheaper, projects that weren't worth doing before become profitable. Think of small businesses getting custom apps, hospitals automating paperwork, and farms using sensor software.

Another labor-demand shifter

Complement or substitute?

AI tools are another input. Their effect depends on how they relate to a given worker:

Complement in production

The AI makes the worker more productive (it raises MP). Cheaper AI → DL shifts right. Example: a developer who designs systems and uses AI to write routine code faster.

Substitute in production

The AI does the worker's task instead. Cheaper AI → DL shifts left (unless the output effect is big). Example: routine code written to someone else's spec.

The same technology can be a complement for one job and a substitute for another. Both effects show up in the official projections →

AP practice · FRQ style

Practice: SoftCo

SoftCo is a profit-maximizing firm that sells apps in a perfectly competitive product market and hires programmers in a perfectly competitive labor market.

(a) State the rule SoftCo uses to decide how many programmers to hire.

Hire until MRP = MRC, where MRC = the market wage (because SoftCo is a wage-taker).

(b) Only SoftCo adopts a new AI tool that raises programmers' MP. The app price doesn't change. What happens to SoftCo's MRP curve, the market wage, and the number of programmers SoftCo hires?

MRP shifts right. The market wage is unchanged because one firm is too small to affect it. SoftCo hires more programmers. (See firm graph →)

(c) Now all firms adopt the tool and the price of apps stays constant. Show the effect on the market for programmers.

Market DL shifts right. The equilibrium wage and the quantity of programmers employed both increase. (See market graph →)

(d) Suppose instead that the price of apps falls sharply, and demand for apps is highly price inelastic. Explain how your answer to (c) could change.

Because MRP = MP × P, a large fall in P can offset the rise in MP. With inelastic demand, quantity of apps rises by a smaller % than productivity, so fewer programmers are needed. DL could shift left, lowering the wage and employment.

Wrap-up

So… does higher productivity kill programming jobs?

It depends, and now you can say on what:

  • MRP = MP × P. Higher MP pushes labor demand right.
  • If all firms become more productive, P falls and pulls MRP back.
  • Elastic demand for the product means employment rises. Inelastic demand means it can fall.
  • Tech that complements a worker raises their labor demand. Tech that substitutes for them lowers it.
  • History and the BLS data show both effects. Software developers grow while routine "programmer" jobs shrink.

Common AP mistakes

  • A change in the wage causes a movement along DL, not a shift.
  • A change in productivity or product price shifts DL.
  • Forgetting MRP = MP × P. Both parts matter.
  • Writing MRC = W in a monopsony. That's true only for a wage-taking firm.
  • Labeling the firm's labor demand curve "D" instead of MRP, or the wage line "W" without MRC.
